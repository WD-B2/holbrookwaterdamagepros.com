How to Save Photos and Documents After Water Damage in Holbrook, NY

Wet paper and photos buy you a short window. These steps protect them while you deal with the rest of the water.

24/7 emergency line
(934) 223-1875Call Now

By Holbrook Water Damage Pros · Published November 13, 2026 · General information, not legal or insurance advice.

Wet photos and papers can often be saved if you handle them gently, keep them from drying stuck together and act within about 48 hours. The first rule is to stop mold before it starts by getting the items out of the damp space. The second is not to peel anything apart by force. This guide lists what to do with photos, paper, books and digital media.

Archivists and libraries publish detailed salvage guidance, including the Library of Congress and the National Archives. The steps below follow the common principles in that guidance. Valuable or irreplaceable items, such as old family photographs, are worth a call to a conservator before you do anything beyond the basics.

Safety and the first hour

Do not enter a flooded area with live power. Water that came from a drain, a flood or outdoors is contaminated, so wear gloves and a mask. Items that touched contaminated water may not be worth saving unless they are irreplaceable, and they should be handled with care.

  1. Photograph the items where they are before moving them.
  2. Move them to a dry, cool room with moving air.
  3. Handle by the edges and do not wipe or rub.
  4. Rinse mud or dirt gently with clean cool water if the item is dirty.
  5. Sort by value so you work on the most important items first.

Photos

Wet photos stick to glass and to each other as they dry. Separate them gently under clean water if needed, then lay them face up on a clean absorbent surface, such as paper towel or clean cloth, away from direct sun. Do not stack. If you cannot do this within a day or two, place them between sheets of wax or freezer paper and freeze them to buy time.

These are common principles. Valuable items deserve expert advice.

Freezing does not fix water damage, it pauses it. Mold needs warmth and moisture, so freezing stops the clock until you have time and space to dry the items properly. A frost-free freezer can dry items slowly, so use a standard chest or stand-alone freezer when you can.

Records and replacement

For documents that cannot be saved, you can usually replace them. Birth certificates, deeds, passports and vehicle titles each have an issuing office. Make a list of what is damaged now and keep it with your insurance records. Photographs of damaged documents also help with a contents claim.

Electronics and drives follow a different set of rules, which another guide covers, and your insurer may want to see damaged contents or photos of them. If the whole room is wet, the first priority is drying the structure. The water extraction page explains how that starts, and the basement water damage page covers storage areas below grade.

Digital backups to set up before the next leak

The cheapest protection is a copy. Scan or photograph the documents you would hate to lose, such as deeds, insurance policies, tax records, wills and medical files, and store them in cloud storage with a strong password. Give a trusted family member access to the folder.

For photographs, a phone scan app or a flatbed scanner works. Scan the oldest and most valuable first. Label files with names and dates so they are useful later. Keep a printed list of account numbers and phone numbers in a waterproof pouch or a safe place away from the basement.

Review the backup once a year. A backup you cannot open is no backup. These steps cost little time and mean a water loss is a nuisance for your papers rather than a loss.

Storage habits that help in Holbrook

Many Suffolk County homes keep boxes in basements and garages, which are the first places to take on water. The National Weather Service recorded 13.57 inches of rain at Islip on August 12 and 13, 2014, with a 12.57-inch report from Holbrook. In a storm like that, items stored on the floor are at the most risk, and items on shelves survive.

A few habits help in any town, from Holbrook to Bayport and West Sayville. Store papers in sealed plastic bins above floor level, scan the most important ones, and keep a copy in cloud storage. After a loss, the habit that matters most is photographing contents before anything is discarded.

Standards and sources

  • ANSI/IICRC S500 is the published standard that restoration crews use to classify water and set drying goals.
  • Rules on insurance, permits and disclosure come from your policy, your town's building office and the State of New York, so confirm with them.

Questions homeowners ask

How long do I have to save wet photos?

About 48 hours before mold becomes likely, and sooner in warm weather. If you cannot dry them in that time, freeze them to pause the damage.

Can I put wet documents in the freezer?

Yes, freezing stops mold and gives you time. Place them in freezer bags or between waxed paper and freeze in small bundles.

Should I use a hair dryer?

No. Heat can warp paper and photos and set stains. Use moving room-temperature air.

What if the water was sewage or flood water?

Contaminated items are a health and cleaning concern. Save only what is irreplaceable and handle it with gloves, or consult a conservator.

What about electronics and hard drives?

Keep them powered off and dry. Do not plug in a wet device. A data recovery service may be able to help.

How do I replace lost documents?

Each document has an issuing office. List what was damaged and contact those offices. Photos of the damage support insurance claims.
