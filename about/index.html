Standards the work follows

Water damage restoration follows ANSI/IICRC S500, the published industry standard for water damage restoration. Mold work follows ANSI/IICRC S520 and the State of New York's Mold Program, which licenses mold assessors and remediators. Guidance on moisture and mold comes from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, which advises drying water-damaged material within 24 to 48 hours.

None of this replaces your own judgment or your insurer's decisions. The pages on this site are general information, written to help you ask better questions, and the guides in the blog go deeper on insurance, cost, mold testing and paperwork. If a page contradicts your policy or a town's rule, the policy and the rule win.

The crews that attend are licensed and insured restoration professionals. We do not publish names, license numbers or ratings on this site, because they would only be accurate for one crew on one day, and the person on the line can answer questions about who is attending.

What we do not promise

  • We do not promise an arrival time. We offer same-day response when available.
  • We do not quote prices over the phone. Water depth, area and materials decide them.
  • We do not promise an insurance claim will be approved. Check your policy or ask your insurer.
  • We do not offer free work, and we do not use forms. The phone is the way in.

What the line is for

The line is for the moment something goes wrong: a pipe, a heater, a washer hose, a roof or a drain. It is also for the day after, when the water is gone and you are not sure what to do about walls and floors that still feel damp. Describe what you see and the person on the line will say what to do next, even if the answer is that it can wait for morning.

Calls to the line may be recorded for quality and training purposes. The line does not use forms, chat widgets or callback queues, because the fastest route to help is a conversation.

What you can do before help arrives

  1. Stop the source if it is safe. Close the fixture valve or the main water valve.
  2. Stay out of water that touches outlets, cords or a panel, and call the utility or 911 for any electrical or gas risk.
  3. Lift valuables, rugs and boxes off the wet floor, and raise furniture legs onto foil or blocks.
  4. Keep children and pets out of water that came from a drain or from outdoors.
  5. Photograph the source, the water and the damaged items before anything moves.

These steps are the same for most losses and are explained in more detail on the water extraction page and the structural drying page. They protect people first and property second, and they keep the claim record clean if you later talk to your insurer.

Why measured drying matters

Water that is left in walls and floors can feed mold, and the EPA says to dry water-damaged material within 24 to 48 hours. A wall can feel dry and still hold water, so crews use moisture meters and thermal imaging and keep daily readings. Those readings, not guesses, decide when the equipment can come out and the rebuild can begin.
