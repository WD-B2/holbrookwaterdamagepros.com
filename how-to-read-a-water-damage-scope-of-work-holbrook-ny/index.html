How to Read a Water Damage Scope of Work in Holbrook, NY

The scope is the contract in plain terms. Know the parts so you can spot what is missing.

24/7 emergency line
(934) 223-1875Call Now

By Holbrook Water Damage Pros · Published November 13, 2026 · General information, not legal or insurance advice.

A water damage scope of work is the written list of what a contractor will do, in which rooms, with which materials and at what price. Reading it carefully before you sign is the best protection you have. This guide walks through the parts of a scope, the units used and the exclusions that surprise people.

The scope is different from the estimate, though the two are often combined. An estimate is the price. A scope is the description of the work. A good scope can be handed to another contractor, who could price the same job from it. If it cannot, it is too vague.

The parts of a scope

Sections of a typical scope
SectionWhat it should sayQuestion to ask
HeaderAddress, date, cause of loss, who is hiredIs the cause stated?
Rooms and areasEach room with dimensions or square footageAre measurements included?
MitigationExtraction, containment, equipment and daysHow many machines and for how long?
DemolitionWhat is removed, by room and materialWho hauls and disposes?
RebuildWhat goes back, with materials and finish levelSame as before, or different?
ExclusionsWhat is not includedWhat would be extra?
ScheduleStart, finish and milestonesWhen is drying complete?
Payment termsDeposit, progress payments, final paymentWhat triggers each payment?

If any section is missing, ask for it in writing.

Units and abbreviations

Scopes use shorthand. SF means square feet, LF means linear feet, EA means each and a day count is used for equipment. Demolition may be listed as 'R&R', which stands for remove and replace. A line that says 'drywall, 2 ft flood cut, 40 LF' tells you how much wall is cut and how high.

Watch for words like 'allowance'. An allowance is a stand-in figure for a cost not yet known, such as flooring. If you choose something more expensive, the difference is yours. Ask how much of the price is allowances and who picks the materials.

Exclusions and what to ask about them

  • Hidden damage found after walls are opened.
  • Mold, asbestos or lead paint if found during demolition.
  • Permits and inspection fees.
  • Contents cleaning or storage.
  • Matching finishes beyond the damaged area.
  • Plumbing, electrical or roof repair that sits outside restoration.

Exclusions are not bad by themselves. They are honest about what is separate. What matters is that you know about them before the work starts. If mold is found, the process shifts to the licensed assessor and remediator described on the mold remediation page.

Change orders and the final walkthrough

A change order is a written approval for work not in the original scope. Insist on one before extra work starts and ask for photos of what was found. At the end, do a walkthrough with the scope in hand and check each line. Ask for the moisture readings that show the structure was dry before rebuild began.

The scope for the dry-out should match the process on the structural drying page, and the rebuild should match what was removed. If the scope removes carpet and pad, the carpet water damage page explains why that happens in some cases and not in others.

A short example of reading one line

Take a line that reads: remove and replace drywall, 2 ft flood cut, 40 LF, includes disposal. It says the contractor will cut the wall two feet above the floor along 40 linear feet, remove the wet board, haul it away and install new drywall. It does not say whether tape, texture and paint are included. That is the question to ask.

Another line might read: set air movers and dehumidifiers, 5 days. Ask how many of each, who monitors them and how you will know it is done. A good answer is a daily moisture log shared with you.

Reading each line this way takes an hour and can prevent weeks of argument. If a line is unclear, ask for a rewrite, not a verbal explanation, so the written scope matches what was agreed.

Scope details that matter in Holbrook

Holbrook is divided mainly between the Town of Islip and the Town of Brookhaven, and the rebuild may need permits from one or the other. The Town of Islip's building division lists a separate permit for plumbing and HVAC work and requires electrical inspection certificates, so check that your scope says who handles those.

Owners in Bohemia are in the Town of Islip and owners in Blue Point are listed in the Town of Brookhaven. A scope should name the town office that covers your address so permits go to the right place.

Standards and sources

  • ANSI/IICRC S500 is the published standard that restoration crews use to classify water and set drying goals.
  • Rules on insurance, permits and disclosure come from your policy, your town's building office and the State of New York, so confirm with them.

Questions homeowners ask

What is the difference between an estimate and a scope?

The estimate is the price and the scope is the description of the work. A good scope could be handed to another contractor to price the same job.

What does R&R mean on a scope?

Remove and replace. It means the item is taken out and a new one is installed.

What is an allowance?

A stand-in amount for something not yet chosen, like flooring. If you pick something costlier, you pay the difference.

Should the scope include moisture readings?

It should say readings will be taken at the start, daily and at the end, and that you can see them.

What if hidden damage is found?

Ask for a written change order and photos before extra work begins.

Who handles permits?

The scope should say who pulls permits for plumbing, electrical or structural work and which town office covers your address.
