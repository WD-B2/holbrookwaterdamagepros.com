What a Sudden Jump in Your Water Bill Can Mean in Holbrook, NY

Most spikes have a simple cause. A few point to a hidden leak that is quietly damaging the house.

(934) 223-1875Call Now

By Holbrook Water Damage Pros · Published November 13, 2026 · General information, not legal or insurance advice.

A sudden jump in your water bill most often comes from a running toilet, an irrigation system, a change in how many people are home or a catch-up bill after months of estimated or zero readings. Less often it points to a hidden leak, such as a break in the underground service line or a pipe inside a wall. This guide shows how to check the cause in an order that starts with the easy ones.

You can learn a lot in 30 minutes with the meter and a few fixtures. Do these checks before you call anyone, and write down what you find. If the checks point to a leak inside the house, the sooner it is found the less damage there is to dry later.

Start with the usual suspects

  1. Compare the billing period. A longer period or a catch-up bill means a bigger total.
  2. Check for a running toilet. Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank and wait 15 minutes without flushing. Color in the bowl means a leak.
  3. Look at irrigation. A sprinkler zone that sticks on can run for days.
  4. Think about recent changes: guests, a filling pool, a new appliance or a garden hose left on.
  5. Check faucets and hoses for drips, including outdoor spigots.

The Suffolk County Water Authority describes a toilet flapper leak as a silent leak that can waste up to 200 gallons a day with no visible damage, and calls running toilets the most common cause of unexplained high bills. It also notes that a bill may be a catch-up bill if earlier readings were estimated or zero because a meter transmitter lost battery power.

Use the meter to find a hidden leak

Step 1

Stop all water use

Turn off faucets, ice makers, irrigation and appliances that use water. Make sure no one flushes.

Step 2

Read the meter

Note the reading or watch the small leak indicator on the dial.

Step 3

Wait 30 minutes

Do not use water in that time.

Step 4

Read it again

If the reading moved or the indicator keeps turning, water is leaking somewhere after the meter.

If the meter moves with everything off, a leak is likely. You can narrow it by closing the main valve inside the house and watching the meter again. If it still moves, the leak is between the meter and the house, often an underground service line. If it stops, the leak is inside the house.

When a leak has already done damage

A leak inside a wall or under a slab can run for weeks before the bill shows it. Signs include warm spots on a floor, damp baseboards, musty smells and bubbled paint. If you see them, close the main, photograph the area and call. The slab leak page and the burst pipe page describe two common hidden sources.

Preventing the next surprise

A few habits catch leaks early. Read your meter monthly and note the number. Look at the leak indicator on the dial when no one is using water. Install water sensors near the water heater, washer and sump pump. Replace old toilet flappers every few years, since they wear out quietly.

Know where your main shutoff is and test it twice a year so it does not seize. If you travel, close the main before you go. A leak in an empty house can run for days.

If your utility offers usage alerts or an online account, turn them on. A jump in daily use is visible in days rather than at the end of the billing period. Early detection means smaller repairs and less drying.

Water service in Suffolk County

Much of Suffolk County is served by the Suffolk County Water Authority, which publishes a high bill guide that walks through toilet leaks, zero consumption and meter readings. Check your bill to see which utility serves your address, and use that utility's guide first. Its guide also suggests looking at underground sprinklers and recent household changes.

The same steps work whether you live in Holbrook, in Bayport or in West Sayville. If you find damage while chasing a bill, the restoration side starts with stopping the source and drying, which the water extraction page describes.

Standards and sources

  • ANSI/IICRC S500 is the published standard that restoration crews use to classify water and set drying goals.
  • Rules on insurance, permits and disclosure come from your policy, your town's building office and the State of New York, so confirm with them.

Questions homeowners ask

What is the most common cause of a high water bill?

A running or silently leaking toilet. The Suffolk County Water Authority calls running toilets the most common cause of unexplained high bills.

How do I test a toilet for a silent leak?

Add a few drops of food coloring to the tank, wait 15 minutes without flushing and check the bowl. Color in the bowl means the flapper is leaking.

What is a catch-up bill?

A bill that covers water used during months with estimated or zero readings, for example when a meter transmitter lost battery power.

How do I check for a service line leak?

Close the main valve inside the house and watch the meter. If it still moves, the leak is likely between the meter and the house.

Who fixes the leak and who handles the damage?

A plumber repairs the pipe. A restoration crew dries and repairs the damage the water caused.

Should I call the water utility first?

If the leak is in the service line outside, yes. For leaks inside the house, a plumber is usually the right call, and a restoration crew if there is damage.
