Commercial Mold Removal in Holbrook, NY: An Owner's Guide

For a business, mold is a moisture problem, a licensing question and a downtime problem at once.

24/7 emergency line
(934) 223-1875Call Now

By Holbrook Water Damage Pros · Published November 13, 2026 · General information, not legal or insurance advice.

Commercial mold removal starts with the moisture that caused it, follows New York's licensing rules for assessors and remediators and is planned around when your business can afford to be closed. Owners and managers who get these three things in order usually avoid the longest delays. This guide explains each, with attention to shops, offices and warehouses in the Holbrook area.

Mold in a commercial building often hides above ceiling tiles, behind wall coverings, under carpet tile or in mechanical rooms. By the time it is visible, the moisture has usually been present for a while. The cause might be a roof leak, a failed pipe, an air handler drain or a past water loss that was never fully dried.

New York's mold program applies to commercial work

New York's Mold Program, administered by the State Department of Labor, requires licensed mold assessors and licensed mold remediators for projects over 10 contiguous square feet. The law separates the roles. The company that assesses and writes the protocol should not be the company that removes on the same project, which protects you from a conflict of interest.

That means a typical commercial job has three parts: an assessor who inspects and writes a plan, a remediator who carries it out, and a clearance check afterward. Ask any contractor to show its state license and verify it with the Department of Labor. Ask for the assessor's protocol before the work begins.

Plan around downtime

Containment keeps spores in the work area, using plastic barriers, negative air machines with HEPA filters and sealed ductwork. That limits how far the work spreads, but the area still has to be off limits. Many commercial jobs are staged by zone so part of the space can stay open, and night or weekend work can limit disruption.

Tell the contractor your hours, your critical areas and any tenants who share systems. In a multi-tenant building, shared air handlers can carry spores between units, so the plan should say how that is controlled. Your lease may set who pays for what, so involve the landlord or property manager early.

Fix the water first

Removing mold without fixing the moisture sends you back to square one. The water side may involve the commercial water damage restoration page's process of extraction and structural drying, along with a roofer or plumber for the repair. The EPA's guidance on mold says to control moisture and to dry water-damaged material within 24 to 48 hours.

The mold remediation page describes the general process for removal, containment and clearance, which applies to commercial and residential work alike. Ask your insurer early about commercial property and business interruption coverage, because mold coverage varies widely.

A planning checklist for owners and managers

  • Name one person with authority to approve scope and schedule.
  • List areas that cannot close, such as server rooms, food prep or patient areas.
  • Ask the landlord and neighboring tenants about shared air handlers and drains.
  • Ask your insurer which policy responds and what the notice deadlines are.
  • Ask the assessor to write the protocol before any contractor bids.
  • Collect moisture logs from any earlier water loss at the property.

Having this list ready speeds the first meeting. It also helps you compare bids, since each contractor is pricing the same written protocol. Without a protocol, bids describe different jobs and the lowest one may leave out containment or clearance.

Keep tenants informed. A short written notice that explains what the work is, where it happens and when it ends reduces complaints and protects you if someone later raises a concern.

Standards and sources

  • ANSI/IICRC S520 is the published standard for professional mold remediation.
  • The New York State Department of Labor's Mold Program licenses mold assessors and remediators for projects over 10 contiguous square feet.
  • The EPA guide A Brief Guide to Mold, Moisture, and Your Home explains how moisture control prevents mold growth.

What to do first, and what not to do

  • Do not enter water that touches outlets, cords or a panel. Call the utility or 911 for any electrical or gas risk first.
  • Do not use a household vacuum on standing water or run fans across suspected mold.
  • Do shut off the water source, lift valuables and photograph the damage.
  • Do keep children and pets out of water that came from a drain or outdoors.

Questions homeowners ask

Do New York mold rules apply to commercial buildings?

The State Department of Labor's Mold Program covers mold assessment and remediation work above 10 contiguous square feet. Ask your contractor to show its state license and confirm it with the Department.

Can one company test and remove the mold?

New York separates the roles on the same project. The assessor writes the plan and the remediator carries it out, so a different company does each.

How long does commercial mold removal take?

It depends on the area, access and whether the water problem is fixed. The plan should give a schedule by zone. Ask for it in writing.

Can my business stay open during removal?

Sometimes, if the work area is contained and shared air systems are isolated. Many projects are staged or done after hours.

Who pays, the tenant or the landlord?

It depends on the lease and the cause. Read your lease and involve your insurer and landlord early.

Does the Holbrook area have special rules?

The Town of Islip or the Town of Brookhaven handles building permits depending on the address. Ask the relevant building office.
